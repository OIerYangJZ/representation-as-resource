Dear Editors,

On behalf of my coauthors, I am pleased to submit the manuscript
``Representation as a Computational Resource in Quantum Compilation:
Approximate Recoverability Tradeoffs and Model-Specific Fault-Tolerant
Consequences'' for consideration in \textit{PRX Quantum}.

Fault-tolerant compilation deliberately destroys structure. Time-dependent and
variational schedules split one accumulated phase across many rounds; gate
synthesis dissolves an angle into a Clifford$+T$ word in which no individual
letter carries the angle; randomized compiling twirls each rotation into
sign-randomized fragments. Each transformation is correct and desirable for its
own reason, and each scatters a quantity that was once written in one place
across an instruction stream. The literature on phase-polynomial, Pauli-gadget,
and ZX methods establishes that a sufficiently capable pass can sometimes
reassemble such a quantity. It does not establish what that reassembly must
cost. This manuscript answers that question.

The central result is a lower bound with two admissible payment channels. We
formalize a downstream compiler---one positioned after randomization and
lowering, as a cloud transpiler or a control-stack stream optimizer is---and
charge every restart channel in literal bits: control, store, live window,
parameters, and intermediate representation. For a commuting phase layer with
$m$ generators compiled to projective accuracy $\epsilon$, any such compiler
making $p$ forward passes and failing with probability at most $\delta$ obeys
\begin{equation*}
  \overline{A}_p + (2p-1)\,S \;\ge\; (1-\delta)\,m \log_2 K_\epsilon - h_2(\delta),
\end{equation*}
where $\overline{A}_p$ is committed output and $S$ the serialized crossing
state. The scattered information must be bought back either in bits of compiler
state or in magic states, and there is no third option. Because a memory-capped
block compiler attains the envelope within a constant factor, the frontier is
tight rather than merely a floor. Charging the intermediate representation
explicitly is what makes the statement non-evadable: a ZX graph, a
phase-polynomial table, a cache, or a spilled update log is an accounted
channel rather than a free side door.

A lower bound of this kind would be of limited interest if its hypotheses were
met only by a constructed secret sharing. They are not. Time-dependent
scheduling supplies the additive decomposition, randomized compiling supplies
the sign symmetry, and the Ross--Selinger synthesis grid supplies the modulus,
so the hypotheses are satisfied by streams that a fault-tolerant stack emits
anyway. Under a disclosed frame-tracked presentation the crossing state is the
Pauli frame itself, which yields a tension we expect will interest the
noise-tailoring community: the randomization that averages coherent noise is
the same randomization that forces a re-aggregating compiler to carry
$\Omega(m \log(1/\epsilon))$ bits. The boundaries of the claim are stated
explicitly; the non-commuting interleaving of a generic Trotter step lies
outside them and is not asserted.

A refinement shows that the two currencies are not worth the same. Once a
committed record is executed rather than buffered, the committed channel
additionally pays $\log_2\binom{m}{k}$---the entropy of the compiler's own
decision about which $k$ coordinates to commit early---while a deferring
compiler is handed that decision for free by the stream that follows. The toll
is independent of $m$ and vanishes exactly when the commitment schedule is
public. It was measured on an instrumented device under a preregistration
frozen before the run, and matches the predicted entropy to within $0.17$ bits
at the worst cell.

The paper does not stop at the information-theoretic statement. Instrumented
reference compilers expose the state, IR, and commitment coordinates directly
at the bit level, and one fixed total error budget is propagated through actual
\texttt{staq grid\_synth} Clifford$+T$ synthesis and executable surface-code
scheduling. Under the disclosed model, the same unitary compiled from a
dispersed rather than an aggregate representation costs $385$--$1{,}753$ times
the logical $T$ states and $538$--$5{,}573$ times the spacetime volume, with no
reversal anywhere in the scanned error and factory grid. The representational
gap becomes a fault-tolerant one.

We believe the work suits \textit{PRX Quantum} because it sits across
communities that rarely share a paper---compiler and IR design, randomized
compiling and noise tailoring, fault-tolerant resource estimation, and
classical communication complexity---and because its conclusion is a design
rule with a proof attached: randomize and lower late. It also identifies
representation as a controlled variable that resource-estimation studies should
report rather than leave implicit. The stance toward existing tools is
explanatory rather than competitive. TKET's \texttt{PauliSimp} and PyZX's
\texttt{full\_reduce} already implement the semantic-first principle on their
global intermediate representations; the contribution here is the bound that
says what any such tool must pay to do so on a dispersed stream. Their recovery
is measured and credited---external TKET uses $0.443$--$0.536$ times the
reference's $T$ states on the same witness---and no headline ranks our
implementation above them. In the same spirit, two preregistered attempts to
carry the commitment toll onto third-party compilers failed, and are reported
as failures rather than repaired.

Every headline number is generated from a normalized frozen-data registry with
a numeric provenance map and a one-command entry point. Correctness is gated by
an independent symbolic or dense certificate, status outcomes are never
converted into numerical wins, and a clean-room runner reconstructs the
theorem, benchmark, and resource-estimation subsets in an independent
directory.

This manuscript is original and previously unpublished. It has not been posted
as a preprint, and it is not under consideration at any other journal. We
declare no competing interests.

Thank you for your consideration. We would be glad to answer any questions.
